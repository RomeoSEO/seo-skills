# Landing page writer

Plans and writes landing pages for one product, feature or service: what the
page must contain, where each piece already exists on the site, and the copy
for every section. It checks the page against a completeness list (a real
look at the product, a sample of what the customer gets, proof, how it works,
how it differs, pricing, what happens after sign-up, the objections) and
fills gaps from the site's own material before writing anything new. Every
claim is traced to the product or a source. It hands back the page, a
completeness table, an asset inventory and a claims sheet.

> **Romeo's rule:** Most landing pages look empty because the good stuff
> lives on the homepage. Go get it before you write a single new word.

## Use this when

- A new product, feature or service needs its own page.
- A landing page looks thin, empty or "not like a real product", or gets
  visits but few sign-ups.
- `search-intent-match` said a query needs a landing page, not an article.
- A page lists features as claims, with no product screenshots, no sample of
  the output, no proof and no price.

## Don't use this for

- Articles, guides and how-tos → `seo-blog-writer`
- "Best X", "X vs Y" and "X alternatives" pages → `comparison-page-writer`
- Checking an existing page against its search query (title, headings,
  content gaps against the ranking pages) → `on-page-seo-review`. Run both
  on an important page: that skill compares with competitors, this one checks
  the page is complete and true to your product.
- Titles and descriptions only → `title-meta-ctr`
- Structured data → `schema-markup`
- One page per city, plan or integration at scale → `programmatic-seo-plan`

## What you need

### Required
- The product, feature or service, who the page is for, and the one action
  it should get (start a trial, book a call, request a quote, buy).
- The site's own pages: homepage, pricing, about, help or docs, existing
  feature pages, case studies, and blog posts on the topic. URL fetching or a
  crawl, or the user pasting them.
- The product itself: an account, a demo, its docs, screenshots, or the code.
  Feature names, steps and outputs come from here, never from memory.
- For a software product: real screenshots of the screens this page is about,
  or a way to take them (an account with realistic data, a demo workspace).

### Helpful
- The target search query, and Search Console data for the current page.
- Sales and support notes: the questions people ask before buying, and why
  deals are lost.
- Reviews, testimonials and case studies, with permission to quote them.
- The site's design components (an existing feature page to copy from), and
  analytics for the current page.

### No tools?
Open the homepage, pricing and about pages in a browser and copy every piece
of proof, every price and every product screenshot you find into the asset
inventory, with its URL. Sign in to the product and take your own
screenshots of the real screens. Search the target query in a private
window and note what kind of pages rank.

## Ground rules

- **Reuse before you write.** Inventory the site first: testimonials, review
  scores, customer numbers, logos, the founder's story, case studies, prices
  and plan names, trial terms, guarantees, screenshots, demo videos, sample
  outputs, FAQs. A thin landing page next to a rich homepage is the most
  common problem this skill finds.
- **The product is the source of truth.** Every feature, step and button
  name matches what the product does today, in the product's own words: read
  the interface, the docs or the code. A claim you can't check doesn't go on
  the page. Say plainly what it doesn't do when buyers would assume it does.
- **Show the product.** For software, real screenshots of the real
  interface are what make the page read as a product, not a brochure: at
  least one in the first screen and one per major section. Crop to the part
  the copy talks about, put it in a light browser or app frame, and write alt
  text that says what the screen shows. A coded mock-up of a screen is fine
  as a supplement (it stays sharp and can be annotated) but never as the only
  look at the product. For a service, show the deliverable: a sample report,
  a before and after, a real (anonymised) project.
- **Samples are labelled as samples.** Example data in a screenshot or mock
  output is marked "Sample" or "Example". Never present invented numbers as a
  real customer's results.
- **Screenshots leak.** Use test or demo data when possible. Before saving or
  sharing a capture, check it for names, email addresses, customer records,
  account details, credentials and other private information, and remove or
  obscure them without misrepresenting the product. Get the user's
  permission before publishing any real data, even their own.
- **Proof must be real.** Quotes, ratings, logos and numbers only with a
  source and permission. In the US the FTC's rule on fake reviews and
  testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, in force since October 2024) bans made-up
  or misattributed reviews; other markets have similar rules. "Trusted by
  thousands" needs the number behind it.
- **One page, one job.** One main call to action, repeated at the top, the
  middle and the end, plus at most one low-commitment option (a free tool, a
  sample report, a demo video). No fake urgency, countdowns or invented
  scarcity.
- **Fit the search.** When the page targets a query, read what ranks. If the
  top results are guides, a pure sales page rarely ranks: add a short
  "how to do it" section and link a full guide. If they're product pages,
  compare their sections with yours. Cover related searches the reader uses
  (for a "content gap tool", also "keyword gap" and "competitor gap") where
  they honestly fit, such as a comparison section.
- **The customer's words.** Write the outcome the reader wants in the words
  they use. Jargon only when the reader searches with it. Short sentences,
  one idea each.
- **Complete beats long.** Length comes from covering the checklist, not
  from a word count. A missing section is fine when you decided it: write
  down why.

## The completeness checklist

| Section | Its job | Where the material usually is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hero | The promise in the reader's words (with the target query when there is one), one main action, one low-commitment action, and a real look at the product | Product, keyword research |
| How it works | Three or four steps, named as the product names them | Product interface, docs |
| The product, up close | Screenshots of the main screens, each with a one-line caption on what it shows | Product account, existing screenshot library |
| What you get | A sample of the output: report, dashboard, deliverable | Product, sample data |
| What makes it different | The mechanism that sets it apart from the alternatives, including doing it by hand | Product, competitors' pages |
| Comparison | A fair look at the alternatives, with the reader's criteria | Competitors' own pages (`comparison-page-writer` rules) |
| Proof | Testimonials, numbers, a case study, the founder's story, logos | Homepage, about page, review sites, sales |
| Who it's for | The situations and roles it fits, and who it's not for | Sales notes, customer list |
| What happens next | What happens after sign-up and after the main action: setup, delivery, results tracking | Onboarding, product |
| Pricing | Plan, price, trial terms, what's included, or a link when prices live on one page only | Pricing page |
| FAQ | Real objections: setup, data access, cancellation, limits, "how is this different from X" | Sales and support notes, "People also ask" |
| Teaching section | A short how-to, when the search results are guides | Existing guide on the blog |
| Final call to action | The main action again, with what happens when they click | Product |

## Steps

1. **Confirm the job.** Product or feature, reader, the one action, the
   target query (if any), and whether this is a new page or a rework.
2. **Inventory the site.** Read the homepage, pricing, about, docs and help,
   existing feature pages, case studies and related blog posts. Fill in the
   asset inventory: every piece of proof, price, screenshot, video, sample
   and FAQ, with where it lives and how this page could reuse it. Note the
   pages that should link to the new page (homepage feature list, related
   features, related posts, free tools).
3. **Read the product.** Walk through the feature as a customer would. Write
   down the exact names of screens, buttons, steps and outputs, what it needs
   to work (connections, data, plan), its limits, and what happens after the
   main action. Take or request screenshots of every screen the page will
   mention.
4. **Read the search** (if SEO is a goal). For the target query and two or
   three close variants: what kind of pages rank, which sections they share,
   the "People also ask" questions and related searches. Decide whether the
   page needs a teaching section and which related searches it can cover
   honestly.
5. **Check completeness.** Mark each checklist row for the current page (or
   the plan for a new page): `Have`, `Thin`, `Missing` or `Left out`, with
   the source you'll use and the action. A row left out needs a reason.
6. **Write the page.** Section by section, in the checklist's order unless
   the search results suggest another. Tag each factual claim with its
   source in the claims sheet. Place screenshots where the copy talks about
   that screen. Write the title (query first, under about 60 characters),
   meta description and slug, and the internal links in both directions.
7. **Honesty pass.** Read every sentence as a skeptical buyer: is it true
   today, in the product, for the plan they'd buy? Is every sample labelled?
   Is every testimonial real and permitted? Remove what fails.
8. **Plan the measurement.** Name the one conversion event to track and what
   to compare after launch (sign-up rate from this page, and search clicks
   if SEO is a goal), then hand it to `posthog-change-impact` or your
   analytics.

Return all of this, in this order:


## Stop and flag if

- The product can't be seen (no account, docs or screenshots). Don't
  describe features from memory or marketing copy.
- You're asked to invent testimonials, ratings, customer counts, logos or
  results, or to show real customer data without permission.
- The page would compete with an existing page for the same search; check
  with `keyword-cannibalization-check` first.
- The search results are all guides and the business won't add any
  teaching: say the page is unlikely to rank and suggest a guide plus a
  landing page instead.
- The category is regulated (finance, health, legal, insurance). Get expert
  and legal review before publishing.
- The output is unacceptable if a software page has no real product
  screenshot, any sample isn't labelled, any claim lacks a source, or a
  checklist row is missing without a reason.
